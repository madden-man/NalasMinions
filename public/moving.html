The studio question

Decided Sep 13: it comes down. Vinyl, panels, and guitar hooks out, holes patched, walls painted. The reasoning and the other options stay here for reference.

Recommendation: still take it down if you'll reuse the mass loaded vinyl at Woods Dr. If you won't, leave the vinyl on the wall and bury it under a new layer of drywall. The video shows hung panels over the vinyl and a room that reads as a dark cave on camera. The basement already has a family room, a gym room, a kitchenette, and a bedroom. At $3,000 a month you're competing with clean 3- and 4-bedroom houses, and a fourth bedroom with a window rents that. The screw holes are real work either way; the section below walks through both repairs.

What restoring it actually involves

  • Unhook the panels (they look hung on hooks or ties, so removal is a morning) and fold them for the move.
  • Fill anchor and hook holes. If the walls under the panels are painted dark, that's one coat of primer and two coats of the house white; if they're already light, touch-up only.
  • Pull the guitar hangers and patch. Clean the carpet with the rest of the basement.
  • Photograph it with the blinds open and a desk in it. It has the window, so it lists as a bedroom or office.

Why not keep it: it would only make sense if you'd rather attract a musician tenant than the widest pool, and even then it photographs badly. Decision made; the options below are the paths not taken.

The vinyl on the walls

Mass loaded vinyl screwed to the studs leaves a fastener hole every foot or two along every stud line, and sometimes torn drywall paper where washers pulled through. That's not a spackle-and-go job, but it's also not a reason to leave the room dark. You chose Option A, strip and patch. The three questions and the drywall-over path stay here in case a lifted panel reveals ceiling vinyl or torn-paper craters that change the math.

Decide with three questions

1Will the vinyl go up again at Woods Dr? It's the expensive part of any future studio, about a pound per square foot and a few hundred dollars for a room this size. Yes: take it down, Option A. No: it's worth more left on this wall than rolled in the garage, Option D.
2Is there vinyl on the ceiling? The video shows panels up there. If vinyl is under them, pulling a pound-per-foot sheet off a ceiling is a two-person misery and ceiling patches show in photos. Ceiling vinyl tips the whole room to Option D.
3How bad are the holes? Peel back one panel and one corner of vinyl this week. Screws with fender washers leave quarter- to half-inch holes: patchable. Toggle anchors, or washers that tore the paper face, leave inch-plus craters and fuzzy paper: still patchable, but at that point drywall-over is faster and looks better.

Option A, step by step

  • Sat Oct 3, morning: take it down. Drill driver, two people, one supporting the sheet while the other backs screws out. Start at the top edge. Roll each sheet as it comes free and label it by wall for reinstalling. Panels and guitar hangers off the same morning. Bag all screws and washers.
  • Sort the holes into three kinds. Screw holes up to half an inch: lightweight spackle, two passes. Half inch to three inches, or a washer crater: self-adhesive mesh patch, three thin coats of joint compound feathered six inches out. Anything bigger or with crushed board: cut a clean square, screw in a wood backer, set a new drywall piece, tape and three coats.
  • Torn paper first. Anywhere the face paper is fuzzy or peeled, seal it with a primer made for torn drywall paper (Zinsser Gardz or similar) before any compound touches it, or the compound bubbles and you do it twice.
  • If the holes are dense, skim instead. More than one hole per square foot means it's faster to skim-coat each wall with a 12-inch knife than to patch individually. Two thin coats, overnight between.
  • Sun Oct 4: second coat and sand. 150 then 220 grit, a light on the wall at a low angle to find ridges. Vacuum the dust; the tile crew is coming Monday.
  • Mon–Tue Oct 5–6, evenings: prime and paint. PVA drywall primer over every patch or the whole wall if you skimmed; compound flashes through paint otherwise. Then two coats of the house white in eggshell. Flat ceiling paint on the whole ceiling if you patched up there.
  • Reassemble. Outlet and switch covers, blinds open, a desk or a made bed for the photo on Oct 9.

Option D, step by step

  • By Fri Sep 18: book it. Ask for half-inch drywall over the existing vinyl, screws long enough to reach the studs, staggered seams, tape and three coats, primed. Get start and finish dates in writing; three to four days because of drying.
  • Before they arrive (Oct 3–4): panels and hangers down, baseboards pried off and labeled, room empty, vinyl seams re-taped flat. Leave the vinyl screwed exactly where it is.
  • What they do (Oct 5–8): hang, tape, mud, sand, prime. Ask them to add outlet and switch box extenders, which code requires once the box sits more than a quarter inch back, and to trim the door and window jambs with a half-inch extension so the reveal looks intended.
  • Optional upgrade while it's open: a bead of Green Glue between the old surface and the new sheet makes it a proper isolation sandwich for another $80–120. Only worth it if you'll market the quiet room.
  • You (Oct 9–10 evenings): two coats of the house white, baseboards back on with a caulk line, covers on. Photo Oct 16 with the new bathroom.
  • In the listing: "Downstairs bedroom with soundproofed walls" in the body copy, not the headline. It's a nice surprise at a showing, not the reason someone comes.
What not to do

Don't paint the vinyl, and don't leave it exposed under a "quiet room" label. Black vinyl walls read as unfinished in every photo, and paint doesn't stick to it well enough to survive a tenant.

Screening: how to find the reliable ones

Write the criteria first, apply them identically to everyone, in the order applications arrive. That is both fair-housing protection and the only way to say no cleanly. Post the criteria in the listing so weak applicants self-select out.

  • Income: household gross of at least $6,000/mo, verified by the two most recent pay stubs plus an offer letter for new jobs, or two years of tax returns if self-employed. Housing vouchers count as income in Colorado and you can't decline them.
  • Credit: set a floor (620 is common) but read the report, not the score. Collections from medical debt are noise; a recent unpaid utility or a prior landlord judgment is signal.
  • Rental history: call the previous landlord, not just the current one. The current landlord may want a bad tenant gone; the previous one has no reason to lie. Ask: paid on time every month, any notices, any damage beyond the deposit, would you rent to them again.
  • Background: eviction filings in the last five to seven years are the main disqualifier. Colorado limits how far back you can look at criminal history; use Zillow's or Avail's compliant report rather than a raw search.
  • Employment: call the employer through the main switchboard number you find yourself, not the number on the application.
  • Identity: ID name must match the application, the pay stubs, and the bank statement. Check that the pay-stub math (gross, taxes, net) actually adds up.

Red flags that predict a bad year

  • Wants to skip the application or "just pay cash up front." Several months of cash offered early is the most common scam and the most common problem-tenant tell.
  • Story arrives before the paperwork. A long explanation of why the credit or the reference is complicated.
  • Landlord reference number that doesn't trace back to the property owner. Look the address up on the Jefferson County assessor site and compare names.
  • Wants to move in this week. Good tenants give notice and plan a month out.
  • More occupants than the application lists, or pushback on the pet policy after the showing.
  • Won't come to a showing but wants to apply sight unseen.

Showings that produce applications

  • Reply to every inquiry within an hour with three pre-screen questions: move-in date, who's living there, pets, and whether $6,000/mo household income and the deposit work. Then offer showing slots.
  • Two open-house Saturdays, Oct 17 and 24, 10am–1pm, plus weekday evenings by appointment. Group showings create urgency and save you twenty trips.
  • Bring the application link on a QR code. Applications within 24 hours of a showing are the ones that close.
  • Keep one sheet: name, date, source, showed, applied, decision. It's your fair-housing record if anyone asks.
Lease clauses worth having
  • Renters insurance required, $100,000 liability, you named as interested party.
  • Rent due on the 1st, paid online; the 7-day grace period and $50 late fee cap are Colorado law, so write them in as-is.
  • Tenant handles lawn, snow within 24 hours (Lakewood code), and furnace filters; you handle everything else.
  • No subletting or short-term rental. Basement kitchenette is not a separate unit.
  • Move-in condition report with your dated photos attached, signed by both sides.
  • Radon disclosure and bed-bug notice attached. Lead-based paint disclosure if the house is pre-1978, which a Lakewood brick ranch almost certainly is.

Self-managing: what it costs you instead

You keep the 8–10% a manager would take, roughly $3,600 a year at this rent, plus a leasing fee. In exchange you own the inquiry inbox for three weeks, two open-house Saturdays, the screening calls, and every maintenance text for the life of the lease. Pick one tool (Avail, TurboTenant, or Zillow Rental Manager) and run everything through it so the paper trail is in one place. Keep a short list of a plumber, an electrician, and a handyman before the tenant moves in; the first call always comes at a bad time.

Colorado rules to build into the lease

Deposit cap, late-fee cap, the 2× income limit, portable screening reports, radon and bed-bug attachments, and the for-cause eviction rules after 12 months are all folded into the listing playbook above. Use your listing tool's Colorado lease and have it reviewed once.
